Image Relay was a digital asset management platform used by 400+ companies worldwide. I joined as their first full-time designer and owned product, research, marketing, and a fair bit of strategy.

Setting up a way to work

Less “what should we build next,” more “what’s the actual problem we’re solving?”

When I rejoined Image Relay full-time in 2016, the team was small, scrappy, and good at shipping. What it lacked was a shared way to decide what to ship and why. I introduced a lightweight design-thinking framework (Understand, Explore, Make, Reflect) alongside project briefs, research, and a roadmap organized around real customer needs.

I also ran annual planning, turning a mission and three strategic pillars into objectives, owners, and Now, Next, Later milestones.

From roadmap to product

A full-day roadmap workshop helped the team map the product’s six core experiences, define three user types (the Governor, Producer, and Consumer) and create a practical path forward around Governance and Sharing, Triggers and Notifications, and Collaboration.

One year, eight launches

In 2017 the small team shipped across brand, marketing, and product, with design behind almost all of it.

Redesigning the core DAM

A full redesign of the digital asset management experience became the product’s pivotal next step. Grounded in user research and iterative customer feedback, we introduced the new system through a controlled client rollout.

The phased launch paired in-product choice with clear walkthroughs, giving teams time to transition on their own terms. Over six months, the redesign moved from pilot to full adoption, while support conversations pointed to strong customer reception and retention remained high.

The problem

Every new version of a layout meant starting the references over.

In regulated healthcare, every claim in a piece, like “9 of 10 doctors recommend Brand X,” needs a numbered reference that cites exactly where it comes from in an official published document. Copywriters place those references on layout PDFs in Acrobat, and nothing carries them forward when the layout changes.

Acrobat was built for reading and commenting on PDFs, not for managing references. So copywriters bend it to fit, drawing each reference by hand with comment boxes and leader lines.

They didn’t need a better workaround. They needed a tool built for the job: nothing more, nothing less.

Pain point 1: a new version arrives

  • Step 1

    The layout shifts.

    Copy is cut, pages move, or the page size changes.

  • Step 2

    References are re-placed by hand.

    Each reference that no longer lines up is moved one at a time, often dozens per piece.

  • Step 3

    It costs hours per piece.

    Copywriters told me it takes two to three hours for a single asset.

Pain point 2: a new piece from reviewed work

  • Step 1

    A new asset is created.

    A copywriter has a new piece, like a flashcard, built from work that was already reviewed.

  • Step 2

    Claims are placed by hand.

    Each reference is copied into the new file and re-added, one at a time.

  • Step 3

    The same work, done again.

    The copy team re-references claims they’ve already referenced on the source piece.

How it works

From two files to a team-ready PDF.

Carryover does one job. It takes the core claims and a layout PDF, places every reference, and hands back a PDF the team can review.

It will never be right every time, so the review screen is the center of the product: it shows where the match is confident, where it isn’t, and lets a copywriter fix anything in seconds.

Where it started

A one-day prototype to give people something to react to.

I built a rough version in about a day. You upload the referenced PDF and the updated one, and it finds where each claim now sits, whether the page size changed or pages were removed. It was crude, but it let me stop describing the idea and start watching copywriters use it.

Research

My first idea came too early.

I assumed copywriters would list their references in a Word doc so the tool could place the pins from scratch. In interviews, they told me they don’t think in Word docs, they want to review work side by side, and some prefer to place every reference themselves.

They also described something I hadn’t scoped: taking the references from a finished piece, like a website, and applying it to a differently structured one, like a flashcard or brochure. That became the second mode, and the one that matters most to the copy team.

Two ways to get references onto a layout

What I assumed copywriters wanted, and what they asked for instead.

Copywriters didn’t want to retype their references into a document. They wanted to see them on the page, next to the layout they already knew.

Iterating as live screens

I iterated the interface as live screens rather than static mockups. In the first days I tried four visual directions side by side in the working tool. None of them became the answer. Seeing them as real screens showed me what each one got right, and I built the style we kept out of that instead of picking one. After that the changes got small: where a control sat, how two of them grouped, which ones could go. The interface settled in about two weeks; testing with copywriters is ongoing.

Four directions, one tool

Each style ran on the same working build, so we compared real screens, not mockups. None of them shipped as they were.

The toolbar, six ways

Most of the work was small: moving a control, grouping two, taking one away.

Proposed workflow

The first idea came back, with better reasons.

When I started working with the developer, he had already begun building around a core claims spreadsheet. We shared it with a new team of copywriters, who agreed it would be easier. Today each copywriter keeps their own claims doc for their own piece, and someone reconciles them by hand.

So I mapped a new flow. One shared core claims document becomes the source of truth. Carryover places those claims on every layout, the copywriter checks them on the same review screen, and the export updates the claims doc for next time.

It also answers the second pain point: one set of claims feeds the website and the flashcard, so nothing is referenced from scratch twice.

Working with AI

AI got me to something testable fast. It also got things confidently wrong.

Claude wrote the code. I directed the product, the interface, and the research.

  • What it sped up

    Days, not months.

    It turned a rough idea into something copywriters could try in days, and showed me design directions as working screens.

  • What it got wrong

    Confident, and sometimes wrong.

    It placed pins with confidence in the wrong spot, and once “fixed” a pin it had gotten right.

  • What it couldn’t do

    The direction came from people.

    It couldn’t tell me which idea was right, or when. That came from the interviews and from working with the developer. Exported PDFs now record what reviewers moved, which I use to tune it.

Outcome

In testing now, with a developer helping turn it into a real product.

Carryover moves claim references from one version of a PDF to the next and is being tested by copywriters on real pieces. I’m working with a developer to take it from a prototype to a full app that saves data and handles proprietary files safely.

The tool works today. The shared workflow around it is the next step.

What’s next

  • Measure

    Record the time saved.

    Pilot carry-over on real pieces and compare against the two-to-three-hour baseline.

  • Validate

    Test the claims flow.

    Walk copywriters through the new flow, then pilot it on a website and flashcard pair.

  • Hand off

    Ready for production.

    Document the matching rules and a regression set, and decide hosting for larger files.

Uploading, part of Image Relay

Improve the first step in an asset’s lifecycle.

Role
Only designer: vision, research, UI, and UX
Team
1 developer, no product manager
Years
2018–2019

Uploading is one of the core parts of an asset management system. But Image Relay’s uploader was last improved in 2012, and it lacked the technical foundation and experience customers had come to expect.

Context

Streamlined, but still familiar

The core of the project was balancing the need for a more streamlined experience without removing too much of the familiarity. Rather than overhauling everything, we approached it iteratively. As a B2B company, it was key to remember that any change also affects how our users get their own jobs done.

Bringing in development, customer service, and account management early helped eliminate as many unknowns as possible.

Focus on clarity

I used a project document to align the team around a few key areas:

Discovery

Understand the current experience

Customer service is the front line of a company. They hear from customers all day, the good, the bad, and the ugly, so I made them part of the design process. They helped audit the issues they heard about most often.

Then I mapped the current experience with screenshots and swim lanes for what users were doing, thinking, and feeling, and layered in the pain points they hit while uploading. We voted on which ones we could solve in this project. Every project has constraints, and you can’t solve every problem at once.

Project goals

Discovery gave us goals to guide the project to the finish line. We made them measurable, because otherwise how do you know you reached them?

  • Fit how people really work

    On desktop or mobile, knowing all of a file’s information or only some of it.

  • Faster uploads

    A stronger technical foundation, and fewer steps to get through.

  • More complete metadata

    Metadata is what sets a DAM apart from basic file storage.

  • More uploads that succeed

    Account for as many ways an upload can stall or fail as possible.

Ideate and prototype

Share early, share often

With pain points, ideas, and goals in hand, it was time to explore. This phase went in a lot of directions, but one key point was to share often. I put rough sketches in front of developers very early, which helped define the constraints we’d need to keep in mind as we built.

Build and beta

Building it together

Collaborating with development during ideation is key, and it’s just as important to stay involved through the build. We wrote spec docs together, with design details, developer requirements, and comments for clarity. That removed as many surprises as possible, but complex projects always surface something.

  • Upload size limits

    Protect the system without getting in the way.

    There were no limits before, which strained the back-end infrastructure. We set limits of 500 files or 5 GB per upload and communicated them clearly.

  • One template per upload

    Fewer options, faster to ship.

    Research showed people upload similar content tied to the same metadata template. Limiting each upload to one put useful constraints on the design.

  • Progressive availability

    Release in stages, from new users to enterprise.

    Brand-new users came first, with no habits from the old uploader. Then we moved up through plan levels, saving enterprise customers, with the most demanding workflows, for last.

Outcome

What shipped

Drag files onto a folder, edit metadata for all of them at once or one at a time, add tags, and keep working while uploads finish in the background, on desktop and on mobile.

Before and after

What I learned

  • Staged releases

    Rollouts doubled as user testing.

    Releasing to groups of customers brought more feedback, faster than recruiting users one by one, and caught bugs before the full release.

  • Browser support

    Check compatibility early.

    Microsoft Edge kept sending us back to problems we thought were solved. The next release explored browser compatibility much earlier.

  • Iteration

    Keep a backlog of improvements.

    A “small” feature can hold very complex flows. You won’t catch them all at launch, so a backlog of iterations is key.

We wanted to think bigger: not just how to make sharing better, but how to go beyond sharing files and help customers present their brand.

Context

Fixing how content gets shared

Image Relay is a digital asset management system: it controls how images, video, audio, and documents reach the people who need them, inside a company and out.

In 2016, as Image Relay’s first and only designer, I inherited a system that hadn’t been updated since 2012. Our first step was to improve one of the core parts of digital asset management: sharing content.

Aligning the team in three days

We had a general idea of where we wanted to go, but I wanted everyone aligned on the same vision from the start. I set up a 3-day workshop with the CEO, a developer, a sales rep, and a librarian, using design thinking and design sprint methods: persona empathy maps, as-is journey mapping, pain points, need statements, and good old team sketching.

It set the direction for the rest of the project: the key pain points, our goals, and an experience flow we could test and build on.

Pain points

The application had plenty of ways to share content, and that was the problem. Sharing one file or one folder was easy enough, but sharing several files got complicated.

  • The wrong default

    Collections became the way to share.

    Sharing via a collection was a very prominent action, so people used it even when it wasn’t the right tool.

  • A buried feature

    Sharing several files was hidden.

    Most people didn’t know they could select multiple files in a folder and share them, so they made collections instead.

  • Clutter

    One-time collections piled up.

    People built an unmanageable backlog of collections they’d created only to share something once.

Project goals

  • Primary goal

    Go beyond sharing a link

    Make collections a place to collaborate, customize, and build branded experiences.

  • Secondary goal

    Fewer one‑time collections

    Keep collections meaningful, and track a drop in how many get created.

  • Secondary goal

    Share across folders

    Share files from several folders without making a collection.

Prototyping and user testing

Testing with both sides of a share

Once we aligned on goals and direction, we started prototyping and putting it in front of users. We focused on two personas: admins, who create collections, and consumers, who receive them, exploring flows and behaviors as well as language.

After several iterations and 9 user tests, we synthesized the findings: key observations went onto sticky notes and were clustered into insights to guide the design. It turned out we were on to something. Pain points were being addressed, and users were excited about the new direction.

Outcome

Curate, collaborate, and communicate, all in one

People now had a way to gather assets for a particular project, presentation, or collaboration. Collections let them view, share, curate, and create.

Before and after

Sharing used to start from a folder in the library. Collections gave it a home of its own.

How it works

From creating a collection to the person receiving it.

Concept, not shipped

Where it was headed

After launch, I explored how collections could grow into a front door for a company’s brand: a collections home that shows what’s working at a glance, and a public portal for partners. These designs weren’t built, but they show where the feedback was pointing.

Ideas waiting in the backlog

What I’d do differently

  • Context

    An older product, wary customers.

    The product was already 10 years old, with design and tech debt, and business customers who tended to be wary of change.

  • Metrics

    Track the results.

    I used Google’s HEART framework to align on metrics, but on such a small team it kept getting pushed aside by other work.

  • After launch

    Make time to revisit.

    Without dedicated product managers and with a constant backlog, we never got back to some of our early decisions.

Where it came from

A small idea worth actually building.

The idea was simple: a maze bigger than the screen, revealed only where you trace, with the view panning along as you go. No brief, no client, no stakeholder. Just a reason to make something for people with a phone, and a way to see how far I could push AI visually.

Finding the game

The first maze worked. It just wasn’t a game yet.

A standard generated maze has one route, so tracing it and solving it are the same act. I asked AI for ways to add challenge without losing the calm, and chose three ideas that worked in layers.

  • Braided mazes

    Loops create real route choices.

  • Fog-of-war

    Unexplored space stays uncertain.

  • Light pickups

    They open and close the fog, so every detour is part of the decision.

Three early directions

Each one tested a different idea of what would make the maze a game.

A game that could change worlds

A new look for each world, not a palette swap.

The hand-drawn and isometric explorations were good, but they competed with what made the game readable: fog, light, and branching routes. The breakthrough was giving every world its own renderer behind a shared system, so each one could play by the same rules without looking like a recolor.

Cutting fog-of-war

The mechanic that started the project stopped earning its keep.

Fog had been in the game since hour two, but every new world needed its own reveal and lighting. Braided mazes and the collection goal were already creating the difficulty. Fog was adding mood, not value, so I cut it, along with the light meter.

That left a gap: nothing tied these different worlds together.

From collecting to hiding

Olly Olly Oxen Free

It’s the hide-and-seek call, but the game had only been about collecting. Turning the collectibles into hidden friends gave every world a reason to exist: the friends needed real places to hide, and someone to find them. Meet Olli and his friends.

Three worlds that ship

Each world has its own levels, built on the same rules.

More levels coming soon

Working with AI, honestly

AI made the experiments possible. Judgment made them worth keeping.

  • What AI did

    It wrote every line.

    It removed the gap between an idea and a test, so I could try a direction instead of debating it.

  • What I did

    Noticed when it felt wrong.

    Too easy, too laggy, too ornate, too forgiving. Naming the problem was the decisive work.

  • What I kept

    Only what made it better.

    I kept what made the game clearer, calmer, and more alive, and cut the rest, even my starting idea.

What’s next

Concept complete, with more to come.

Olli ships three worlds today: hedge, cave, and a forgotten village, with a hiding mechanic that finally matches the name. More worlds are coming, and an App Store release is next.

I was a design lead, part of a cross-functional team of designers, engineers, and product managers working to solve complex problems at scale.

The studio

Solving complex problems at scale.

More specifically, a predictive analytics product built for the manufacturing industry, helping make smarter operational, maintenance, and repair decisions by turning complex data from multiple sources into something actually usable.

The work focused on running workshops, design sprints to align stakeholders, and validating concepts through real user testing. Beyond the product itself, I got the opportunity to lead internal workshops, which allowed me to help educate designers, PMs, and engineers about design thinking methods.

Hills workshop

Over several days I planned and facilitated a workshop with 18 colleagues across product, engineering, sales, and design. The agenda pulled from three places. IBM’s Hills Workshop set direction, Google’s Design Sprint pushed the group toward fast convergence, and general design thinking activities kept us grounded in real user needs.

A Hill is a statement of user outcome. It names who you are serving, what you want them to be able to do, and what makes your solution distinctive. IBM calls these the Who, the What, and the Wow. The point of a Hill is to commit to a destination without locking in the route, so the team stays aligned without prematurely closing off ideas.

The workshop set the structure for everything that followed. It produced a Hills playback that became the team’s shared reference point, and from there the project moved into a series of 3-0 playbacks that kept the work tracking against those original outcomes.

Playback −3

This was the kickoff playback for A&O, a predictive analytics layer built into IBM’s Predictive Maintenance & Quality product. At this stage we were intentionally rough, sharing two to three concept directions in sketch form alongside the scenario and hills we’d been building toward. The point wasn’t to land on a solution. It was to align the team on who we were designing for, surface the assumptions we were making, and pull engineering into early conversations about epics and effort before any of us got attached to a direction.

What we showed at Playback −3

The scenario, then rough concepts in sketch form.

Playback −2 and −1

Narrowing toward one direction

Two more playbacks sat between these, each with a round of user testing. Over those rounds the concept set narrowed to a single direction, the scope tightened from two personas to one, and the fidelity climbed as decisions firmed up. By PB-0 we weren’t asking what this could be. We were asking whether this was the thing we wanted engineering to build.

Playback 0

This was the final playback in the cycle, where we moved from rough concepts into a connected set of high-fidelity wireframes organized around the user stories engineering would build against. The fidelity was high enough to make flows feel real and pressure-test interaction patterns, but the visual language was still version 0.1, not a finished design. The point was to validate the experience end to end before sprint planning, lock in story scope with engineering, and surface the last open questions about behavior, hierarchy, and edge cases.
